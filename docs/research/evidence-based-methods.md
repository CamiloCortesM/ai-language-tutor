# Learning English (L2) as an adult Spanish speaker: evidence-based practices

> Project research base (2026-07). Grounds the tutor's methodological decisions.

## 1. Comprehensible input (Krashen)

**What it is:** language is acquired by understanding input slightly above the current level (*i+1*), subconsciously, with a low "affective filter" (low anxiety). Krashen separates *acquisition* (implicit) from *learning* (conscious) and minimizes the role of output.

**State of the evidence:** comprehensible input is a **necessary but not sufficient condition**. Lichtman & VanPatten (2021) conclude that 40 years on, Krashen's core intuitions hold (input drives acquisition), but his strong formulations do not. Solid, current criticisms: *i+1* was never defined measurably; denying output is empirically untenable; acquisition is not purely passive but **active, interactive and multimodal** (neuro-ecological critique, Frontiers 2025).

**Application:** consume large volumes of comprehensible material (95%+ known words) that is slightly challenging. It is the foundation, not the whole method.

## 2. Output hypothesis (Swain) and production

Producing (speaking/writing) does three things input alone cannot: **1)** it forces the jump from semantic to syntactic processing; **2)** it triggers *noticing* (the learner detects gaps between what they want to say and what they can say); **3)** it allows hypothesis testing and feedback. Current consensus: input + **negotiated interaction** + output beats input alone. Speaking early, even imperfectly, accelerates fluency and consolidates forms.

## 3. Paul Nation's Four Strands

A balanced course spends **~25% on each strand**:

| Strand | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning-focused **input** | listening/reading for meaning | extensive reading/listening, podcasts, series |
| Meaning-focused **output** | speaking/writing for meaning | conversation, journals, summaries |
| Language-focused **learning** | deliberate study of form | vocabulary (SRS), explicit grammar, pronunciation |
| **Fluency** development | using known language fast and effortlessly | 4/3/2, timed reading, repetition |

Key rule: in the three meaning strands the material must be **easy** (fluency and comprehension); only the language-focused strand introduces difficulty. The most common self-learner mistake is spending 80% on language-focused work (grammar/vocab apps) and almost nothing on fluency and real output.

## 4. Vocabulary: required size and spaced review

**Coverage thresholds (word families, Nation):**
- 2,000 families ≈ 80% of a text; **3,000 ≈ 95%** (functional threshold to start reading/listening extensively).
- **98% coverage** (comfortable comprehension without a dictionary): ~**8,000–9,000** families for written text, ~**6,000–7,000** for speech.

**Receptive vocabulary per CEFR level** (Milton, lemmas, indicative):

| Level | Approx. receptive vocab |
|---|---|
| A1 | ~1,500 |
| A2 | ~1,500–2,500 |
| B1 | ~2,750–3,250 |
| B2 | ~3,250–3,750 |
| C1 | ~3,750–4,500+ |

Note: Spanish speakers start with an advantage — thousands of Latin/academic cognates in formal registers — but the high-frequency Germanic vocabulary (phrasal verbs, short words) must be learned like anyone else.

**Review evidence:** the **testing effect / retrieval practice** (actively recalling > re-reading) plus the **spacing effect** are among the most robust findings in learning psychology. SRS systems that surface difficult cards more often consistently beat massed review and paper flashcards. **Actionable:** SRS with active recall, cards with audio and in-context examples (not isolated word→translation), 15–20 min/day.

## 5. Concrete techniques with support

- **Extensive reading/listening:** the engine of the input strand. Material at 98% known words, for pleasure, in volume. Improves vocabulary, reading fluency and implicit grammar.
- **Sentence mining:** extracting real sentences (from shows/reading) into SRS cards. No direct RCTs, but it combines what does work: context, collocations, spaced retrieval. Recommended.
- **Shadowing:** repeating in near-real time, imitating native audio. Evidence (mostly Japanese EFL) is positive for prosody, perception/listening and fluency. Useful for pronunciation and automatization.
- **Chorusing:** modeled repetition of phrases; helps fix articulatory patterns and intonation.
- **4/3/2 (fluency, Nation):** give the same mini-talk to three listeners in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. Increases speech rate and reduces hesitation (constant content, growing time pressure). Excellent for fluency development.
- **Error correction (feedback):** corrective feedback **works and is durable** (meta-analysis Lyster & Saito 2010). **Prompts** (cues that push the learner to self-correct) tend to beat **recasts** (implicit reformulation), especially for grammar, because they generate production and *noticing*. In writing, **focused written corrective feedback** (few error types at a time) beats unfocused. More explicit feedback → better for adults.

## 6. Pronunciation: perception and production

- **Perception first:** **HVPT (High Variability Phonetic Training)** has the strongest evidence. Meta-analysis (79 studies): **medium-large effects** (g ≈ 0.92 pre-post; g ≈ 0.67 vs. control), with **long-term retention** and **generalization** to new words and voices. It consists of discriminating target sounds produced by **multiple speakers** in varied contexts, with **immediate per-trial feedback**. **Minimal pairs** (ship/sheep, bit/beat) train the distinctions the Spanish ear does not perceive.
- **Production:** shadowing and chorusing; explicit articulatory feedback; recording yourself and comparing. Training perception before production outperforms producing blind.

## 7. Grammar: explicit vs. implicit

Reference meta-analyses (Norris & Ortega 2000; replication Goo et al. 2015; Spada & Tomita 2010): **explicit instruction beats implicit** in effect sizes, for both simple and complex forms, and the effects are **durable**. *Focus on form* (attention to form **inside** communicative tasks, at the moment the need arises) vs. *focus on formS* (isolated rule study) yields large and **similar** effects; what works worst is purely implicit exposure with no attention to form at all.

**Actionable for an adult:** short doses of explicit rule explanation + lots of practice in communicative context (*focus on form*). Don't spend months in grammar books; do resolve specific doubts with a clear explanation and then use the form.

## 8. Estimated hours A1 → C1

**Cambridge / Council of Europe (cumulative guided learning hours):**

| Level | Cumulative guided hours |
|---|---|
| A1 | ~90–100 |
| A2 | ~180–200 |
| B1 | ~350–400 |
| B2 | ~500–600 |
| C1 | **~700–800** |
| C2 | ~1,000–1,200 |

**FSI:** for an English speaker, Spanish (Category I) requires **~600–750 classroom hours** to ILR 3/3 (≈ C1). The reverse case, Spanish→English, is comparable since it's the same close language pair.

**Practical figure:** plan for **~700–900 guided hours to C1**. That number assumes instruction/deliberate study; adding passive input (series, podcasts) makes total exposure much larger. **Adjustment for Spanish speakers:** a *discount* on academic vocabulary and syntax (close language) but a *surcharge* on phonology and the vowel system/absent phonemes. Realistic pace at 1 h/day ≈ 2–3 years to C1.

## 9. Typical errors of Spanish speakers

**Phonology (the most expensive part):**
- **Vowels:** Spanish has 5 vowels; English ~12. Classic failure: not distinguishing /ɪ/–/iː/ (*ship/sheep*), /æ/–/e/ (*man/men*), /ʌ/–/ɒ/. Priority #1 for HVPT/minimal pairs.
- **Initial /s/ + consonant clusters:** epenthetic vowel insertion → "estart", "esport", "estrike".
- **Consonants:** /b/–/v/ merged; /θ/, /ð/ (*think, this*) substituted; /dʒ/–/j/; final devoicing; coda simplification (*task*→"tas").
- **Rhythm:** English is *stress-timed* (unstressed syllables reduce to schwa /ə/); Spanish is *syllable-timed*. Working on vowel reduction and word stress is key for intelligibility.

**Grammar:**
- Dropping the subject (*"Is raining"* for *"It's raining"*) — Spanish allows it, English doesn't.
- Adjective-noun order (*"a car red"*).
- Third-person *-s* in the present; articles (a/an/the); present perfect vs. past simple; *do*-support in questions/negations; prepositions (*depend on*, not *depend of*).

**False friends:** *actually*≠actualmente, *library*≠librería, *exit*≠éxito, *assist*≠asistir, *college*≠colegio, *carpet*≠carpeta, *embarrassed*≠embarazada, *realize*≠realizar, *sensible*≠sensible (=sensato).

## Actionable synthesis
1. **Foundation:** massive comprehensible input (extensive reading/listening at 98%).
2. **Balance the 4 strands (~25% each);** don't neglect real output and fluency.
3. **Vocabulary:** SRS with active recall and context; target 3,000 families to read, 8,000 for mastery.
4. **Pronunciation:** HVPT + minimal pairs for perception before production; focus on vowels and /s/ clusters.
5. **Grammar:** explicit but brief, inside communicative tasks (*focus on form*).
6. **Feedback:** correction via *prompts* (self-correction) over passive reformulation.
7. **Hours:** ~700–900 guided to C1; ~2–3 years at 1 h/day.
